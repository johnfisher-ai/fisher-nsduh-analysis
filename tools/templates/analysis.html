Summary

Three survey years pooled give {pooled} records, of which {n} adults have a usable depression outcome. Weighted to the population, {mde_w}% report a past-year major depressive episode; the unweighted figure is {mde_u}%.

A penalised logistic regression on seven predictors reaches AUC {auc}. At the default cut-off it is {acc}% accurate and flags {pos} people out of {n}, which is the clearest demonstration on this site of why accuracy alone is not a useful measure.

Ignoring the survey design also understates standard errors by a factor of roughly {se_min} to {se_max}, so confidence intervals computed without it are around half as wide as they should be.

Nearly nine in ten of the dropped records are adolescents who were never eligible to answer. Treating that as missing data would be a mistake: it is a restriction on the population. Every result here describes adults, and any statement about "Americans" that includes teenagers would be wrong on this evidence.

Pooling three years also requires dividing each respondent's weight by three. Without that, the weighted totals describe a country three times too large.

2. The survey design, and what ignoring it costs

NSDUH over-samples some groups by design. A respondent's weight says how many people they stand for, and the stratum and replicate identifiers describe how the sample was drawn. All three ship with the public-use files.

It moves the estimates

{prev_rows}
Prevalence among adults, computed two ways from the same respondents.
MeasureUnweightedWeightedDifference

Depression and marijuana move most, both downward: {mde_u}% to {mde_w}% and {mj_u}% to {mj_w}%. Both are behaviours concentrated in younger adults, and younger adults are over-represented among respondents.

The mechanism is visible here. Within an age band the two figures almost agree. It is the mix of ages that differs between the sample and the country, so only the overall figure moves. That is what weighting is for, and it is why an unweighted national rate can be wrong even when every subgroup rate is nearly right.

It changes how precise the estimates are

Respondents are drawn in clusters rather than independently, so they carry less information than the same number of independent draws would. A model that ignores this reports standard errors that are too small.

{se_rows}
Standard errors from the same logistic regression, with and without the design.
PredictorNaive SEDesign SERatio

Every standard error grows by between {se_min} and {se_max} times. A confidence interval built on the naive figures is roughly half the width it should be, and a p-value computed from them is correspondingly optimistic. Nothing here reverses a conclusion, because the effects are large and the sample is enormous, but the reported precision would be wrong.

The design-adjusted figures use the stratum and replicate identifiers as the clustering unit. This captures the clustering and the weighting but not the gain from stratification, so it is approximate and tends to be conservative. Read the ratio as the rough scale of the error, not an exact design effect.

4. Reducing and grouping

Seven measures were standardised and reduced by principal components. The first three carry {pca3}% of the variance, which is the basis for grouping respondents.

{pca3}% across three components is modest. These are seven fairly distinct measures, so there is no dominant single dimension to find, and the components should be read as a convenient summary rather than as latent traits.

One group separates sharply: {mj_cluster_pct}% of the sample with {mj_cluster_mj}% reporting past-month marijuana use, and a depression rate of {mj_cluster_mde}%, well above the others. The remaining three are distinguished more by age, income and health than by substances.

Clusters are a description, not a discovery. K-means returns four groups from any dataset, including one with no group structure at all. Refitting on twenty random subsamples returns cluster sizes of about {stab}, stable to within {stab_sd} percentage points, so the partition is reproducible. Reproducible is not the same as real: these may be genuine types of people, or convenient cuts through a continuum. Nothing here distinguishes the two.

5. Predicting depression, and the trap in accuracy

A penalised logistic regression on the seven predictors, evaluated by cross-validation, reaches an AUC of {auc}. AUC is the probability that a randomly chosen person with depression is ranked above a randomly chosen person without it, so {auc} means the model carries real signal.

Now the same model, judged by accuracy. It is {acc}% accurate. It also flags {pos} people out of {n} as depressed, when {mde_u}% of the sample actually report an episode. Cohen's kappa is {kappa}, effectively zero.

The model achieves {acc}% accuracy by predicting "not depressed" for almost everyone. With an outcome that occurs in about one in nine adults, always guessing "no" is right about eight times out of nine. Accuracy rewards that. It is not a useful model at this cut-off, and accuracy is the metric that hides it.

The failure is in the decision rule, not the model. The ranking is good; the default 0.50 cut-off is simply far above where the probabilities live for an outcome this rare.

{thresh_rows}
One model, several cut-offs. The shaded row is the default.
Cut-offSensitivitySpecificity PrecisionKappaFlagged

At 0.50 the model finds {sens50}% of people with depression. Lowering the cut-off to 0.20 finds {sens20}% of them, at the cost of flagging {flag20}% of everyone for a closer look. Which trade-off is right depends entirely on what happens to a flagged person: a cheap follow-up question justifies a low threshold, a costly intervention does not.

The imbalance is {imb} to 1. Class weighting and resampling are the other standard responses, and they move the same trade-off along the same curve rather than creating new information.

6. What this supports, and what it does not

Supports. Among U.S. adults, past-year depression is strongly patterned by age, sex and self-rated health. Marijuana use is the substance measure most associated with it. A small group of heavy multi-substance users carries a markedly higher rate. These associations are precise, because the sample is very large.

Does not support. Any causal reading. This is a cross-sectional survey: it cannot say whether substance use precedes depression, follows it, or both share a cause. The measures are coarse, self-reported, and binary in this analysis: "any use in the past month" treats a monthly drink and daily heavy use as the same thing.

Does not address. Adolescents, who are excluded by construction. Treatment, severity and duration, none of which are in these variables. And the great majority of what actually drives depression: genetics, life events, social support and care history are not in the model, which is why an AUC of {auc} is the ceiling here rather than a disappointment.

7. How this was computed

All figures are computed from the SAMHSA public-use files with pandas, scikit-learn and statsmodels, using only the columns needed rather than all 2,650. Random seeds are fixed at 1; changing them moves the cluster labels and the last digits of the cross-validated figures.

The data is not in the repository. Aggregate results are written to tools/derived/, which is committed, so every page rebuilds without it. See the code page.
